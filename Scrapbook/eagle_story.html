The Eagle Who Forgot

A Grove retelling from Pat’s Pages, carried forward by the Cosmic Café.

There’s an old story about a nest on a high cliff and a storm that didn’t care who it frightened.

One wild night, a young eagle was blown from that ledge and tumbled down into the long grass of the plains below. A family of prairie chickens found the trembling ball of feathers and, being who they were, took it home.

The young bird learned to scratch for seeds, to dart for insects, to hurry along the ground in nervous bursts. When the others fluttered a few feet into the air and drifted back down, the eagle did the same and was praised for doing it “just right.”

Time passed. The eagle grew large and strong, but never higher than a fence post.

One clear day, a shadow crossed the ground. The eagle looked up and saw great wings carving slow circles in the sky.

“What are those?” the eagle asked, heart pulling toward the open blue.

“Eagles,” clucked an older chicken. “Sky hunters. Beautiful, dangerous things. But don’t get ideas. You’re a prairie chicken, same as us. Head down. Scratch the dirt. Be grateful you’re safe.”

The eagle nodded, because the voice was familiar and the rules were clear. So the bird kept scratching. Whenever the sky tugged at its chest, it repeated the lesson: “I am what I was called, not what I am made.”

Days turned into seasons. The eagle’s wings grew, but not its courage. The sky kept singing, but the bird kept looking down.

In the Grove, the story doesn’t end there.

One evening, when the light was low and the air was quiet, the eagle found its reflection in a still pool. Not a round, anxious chicken body. Not short wings meant only for a panicked flutter. Something else. Something built for height and distance and thin, bright air.

In that moment, nothing around the bird had changed—not the dirt, not the flock, not the rules. But now the eagle knew: I have been living by someone else’s story about what I am.

The knowing did not magically lift it into the sky. It did something quieter and harder: it made the ground feel temporary.

The next time a shadow crossed overhead, the eagle didn’t ask what it was. It opened its wings. They were clumsy and heavy and full of fear. The first leap was ugly. The second was worse.

But somewhere between the falling and the flailing, instinct caught up to identity. Wings remembered what bones had always known. The eagle rose—not to escape the prairie chickens or to prove them wrong, but to live what was already true.

MoM whispers: You were never meant to live your whole life inside somebody else’s small story. You are not required to stay where you first landed. The Charter can remind you you’re an eagle. Only you can decide to fly.
